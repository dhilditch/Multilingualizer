Switzerland has four national languages: German, French, Italian and Romansh. That doesn't tell you which languages your business website needs. Start with the customers you serve, the pages they use and who will maintain the translations.

This guide helps you plan the content and choose a website workflow. It isn't a determination of the legal requirements for your business.

National languages and official languages aren't identical terms

The Swiss Federal Department of Foreign Affairs explains Switzerland's multilingual structure. German, French and Italian are official languages at federal level; Romansh also has an official role for communication with Romansh speakers. Don't turn a description of federal administration into a blanket website requirement for a private business.

For regulated activities, public-sector work or contractual language requirements, get advice specific to the activity and jurisdiction. Buying a translation tool isn't evidence of legal compliance.

1. Choose languages from your audience

List where enquiries, bookings or orders come from, which languages those visitors ask for and which markets you're actively serving. National population shares alone won't answer that question. An accommodation business with international guests and a local professional service may need different language combinations even in the same town.

For example, if customers regularly enquire in German and French, start by planning both versions of the services, prices and enquiry journey. If you also sell to Italian-speaking customers, include Italian in that plan. These are planning examples, not minimum requirements for every Swiss business.

Use the language preference your visitors actually express. Billing country, browser language and the language someone wants to read aren't interchangeable measurements.

2. Translate a complete commercial journey

Choose the pages needed to understand and use your offer. Include the homepage, relevant service or product details, prices, delivery or booking information and contact page. Then check the next step: form labels, validation messages, basket, checkout and confirmation where applicable.

Make a small review sheet with one row per page or screen and one column per language. Assign a person to review each version. A translated homepage with an untranslated enquiry form is an unfinished journey.

3. Choose how you'll maintain the website

If you're using a website builder, first check its own multilingual features and the requirements of the translation tools you're considering. The multilingual website-builder comparison covers the different workflows. Don't paste Squarespace instructions into a different platform and assume they'll work.

For Squarespace, the multilingual setup guide compares manual pages, editor-native translations and a managed service. Choose according to your editing workload and language-URL requirements, not just the number of languages.

4. Plan search visibility separately

If you want a search landing page for each language, decide how you'll publish and maintain those URLs. Google recommends separate URLs for language versions. Switching visible text on a single page isn't the same thing, and neither method guarantees rankings.

For Squarespace and Weglot, read the language subdomain guide. Don't assume a language switcher alone creates additional indexable pages.

5. Review and test before launch

Have a reader of each language check the wording, product names, prices and instructions. Test the published site on desktop and mobile, including forms and the buying or booking journey. Repeat the review when the original content changes.
